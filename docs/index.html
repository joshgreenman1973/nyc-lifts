Buttons

what these numbers do not press

Every figure here comes from the Metropolitan Transportation Authority's own published data. That is a strength and a limit at once. What the record does not capture:

  • An elevator counted as available can still fail you. Availability is measured in hours in service. It does not measure whether the car was filthy, whether the door held, or whether it was running when you happened to arrive.
  • "No way in" is deliberately conservative. A station appears only when every elevator marked accessible there is out in the live feed. Many complexes need several elevators in sequence to get from street to platform, so a single outage can strand a rider at a station this page still counts as open.
  • Two MTA sources disagree, and this page takes the cautious side. The equipment inventory and the live outage feed do not describe the same fleet. Calling a station inaccessible is the strongest claim here, so it is made only where the continuously updated live feed reports every accessibility elevator at that station out, and only at stations the agency itself lists as accessible. Accessibility elevators currently in that gray zone: .
  • What counts as an elevator is decided by behaviour, not by a label. The inventory's own status field cannot be trusted for this: seven units it marks as removed filed 94 to 99 percent availability last month, and every unit it marks installed-but-not-functioning filed nothing. So the fleet here is the elevators that actually reported service hours in the most recent month. Units listed as out that are not filing at all, generally long capital replacements, are shown in gray and counted separately.
  • Some equipment cannot be placed. - inventory records carry no station at all, and - elevators never appear in the availability filings. They are excluded from station level figures rather than guessed at.
  • "Redundant" is a weaker word than it sounds. The inventory records whether some other elevator backs a unit up, and where it says yes it almost always names the specific machine (- of -). But that is a statement about the building, not about your trip: it does not mean the backup serves the same platform, the same direction, or that it is working today. Half the fleet serves a single direction of travel, and - elevators both serve one direction and have no backup at all — when one of those stops, riders heading that way have nothing. Where the field is blank, this page makes no claim.
  • Entrapment counts are reports, not injuries. The data records that riders were shut inside and freed. It says nothing about how long they waited or what happened to them.
  • Most of the subway is not in this story at all. Step-free access exists at a minority of stations. A perfect reliability score would still leave most of the system unreachable.

How this was built

Six public feeds, refreshed automatically. Full detail is in the methodology.

  • Live outages come from the agency's public elevator and escalator feed, read directly with no key.
  • Reliability comes from monthly per-unit availability filings beginning January 2015. Percentages are recomputed from summed hours available over summed hours total. Averaging the published per-unit percentages would let a unit tracked for one month count as heavily as one tracked for eleven years.
  • Accessibility status and redundancy come from the station complex list and the equipment inventory, joined on the agency's station complex identifier. The join was verified by spot check before use.
  • Ridership is the trailing twelve months of complex level counts, used only to describe how many trips a closure touches.

Everything substantive here (the concept, the choice of data, the questions asked of it and the desired layout) was directed by the author, a human. The coding, the data plumbing and the file wrangling were primarily in the hands of Anthropic's Claude and Claude Code, under close and skeptical direction.

Quality control actually performed on this project: the station identifier join was verified by spot check against named stations before any figure was computed; availability was recomputed from summed hours rather than averaged percentages; entrapment totals were reconciled against a server side sum of the source dataset; equipment that has been physically removed was separated from equipment that is merely broken, after a discrepancy between two MTA files was found and traced; the fetch scripts fail loudly and refuse to publish rather than write an empty or truncated file; and every headline number was independently recomputed from the raw files by a reviewer working blind, with no knowledge of how the page was built.

The risks of using AI remain. If you spot a red flag in anything presented here, please say so.
