How availability is calculated

The source publishes, for each unit and each month, hours available and hours total. Every percentage on this site is recomputed as the sum of hours available divided by the sum of hours total across whatever group is being described.

It is not the average of the published per-unit percentages. That distinction matters: averaging ratios would let a unit that reported for a single month weigh as heavily as one that reported for eleven years, and would let a brand new elevator installed in December pull a whole year's figure around.

Partial years

The latest calendar year is incomplete. It is drawn with a hollow marker on the availability chart and an outlined bar on the entrapment chart, and it is excluded from any "latest full year" figure quoted on the front page.

What counts as an elevator

This sounds like a formality and is the single most consequential decision on the site.

The equipment inventory publishes a service status for every unit: IFIS in service, INOS and IFOS installed but out of service, RNOS removed. The obvious approach is to treat that field as the truth. It is not reliable:

  • Seven elevators the inventory marks removed filed 94 to 99 percent availability in the most recent month. They are plainly running.
  • Every unit marked INOS or IFOS filed nothing at all.
  • 62 elevators the inventory lists have never appeared in the availability filings in any month since 2015.
  • 23 inventory records carry no station assignment at all.

Note also that IFOS expands to "Installed-Functioning-Out of Service", so "installed but not functioning" is the wrong gloss for it.

Presence is therefore behavioural: an elevator is treated as part of the operating fleet if it filed service hours in the most recent month of availability data. That is a statement about what the machine did, not about what a static file says it is. On that definition the fleet is the elevators reporting in the latest month, and every fleet-wide figure on the front page uses it, including the redundancy denominator and the test for whether a station has lost step-free access.

Units that appear in the live outage feed but are not filing service hours, generally multi-year capital replacements, are counted and displayed separately in grey. They are real losses of service and are not hidden; they are simply not counted as breakdowns of machines that are running.

When a station counts as having no way in

A station complex appears in "No way in" when all three of these hold: the MTA's own station list records the complex as accessible, in full or in part; every accessibility-flagged elevator there that is currently filing service hours is listed as out in the live feed; and the complex has at least one such elevator.

The first condition matters more than it looks. A few complexes that the agency records as having no step-free access nonetheless carry an accessibility-flagged elevator in the equipment file. Without that check the page could announce "no way in" at a station its own map draws as never having been accessible, which would be both wrong and self-contradicting.

This is deliberately conservative, in two directions worth stating plainly.

It undercounts. Many complexes require several elevators in sequence to get from street to platform. One broken elevator in that chain can strand a rider at a station this page still counts as open, because another accessible elevator elsewhere in the complex is running.

It resolves conflicts between two MTA files in favour of the cautious answer. The inventory and the live feed do not describe the same fleet, and where they disagree the continuously updated feed decides, because calling a station inaccessible is the strongest claim this site makes.

Joins

The availability and inventory files identify stations by a complex reference number; the station list uses a complex identifier. These are the same numbering, which was confirmed by spot check against named stations (complex 451 resolves to Junction Blvd and complex 146 to 181 St in both files) before any figure was computed. Every equipment code in the live feed resolves to a record in the inventory, with no unmatched units.

Ridership is joined on the same complex identifier. Of 445 complexes, 426 have ridership in the trailing window; the remainder are shown without a trip count rather than with a zero.

The eleven year grid

One row per elevator that has ever filed availability data, one cell per month, ordered by borough and station. 62 inventory elevators never file and cannot be drawn. Each cell is bucketed by that month's availability. Blank cells are distinguished by cause: before a unit's first filing, after its last, and gaps in between, which are not the same thing and are labelled separately in the tooltip. The buckets are deliberately fine at the top end and coarse at the bottom, because most unit-months sit between 98 and 100 percent and that is where the differences are legible.

Escalators are excluded from the grid. They are a different machine answering a different question, and mixing them would double the rows while diluting the accessibility story. They remain in the availability chart, drawn separately, and on the outage bars.

Entrapments

An entrapment is an event in which one or more riders were shut inside a stopped elevator. The figure is the agency's own count of events, not of people: the data does not record how many riders were in the car, so the total is a floor on the number of people affected and the site labels it as times riders were shut in rather than as a headcount. Escalators record effectively none across the whole period, which is what you would expect and which is a useful check that the field means what it appears to mean.

The chart offers a per-100-elevators view because the fleet grows over the period. Absolute counts rising while the fleet grows is a weaker claim than the rate rising, and the toggle lets a reader see which is happening.

What this cannot tell you

  • Whether an elevator was working at the moment you needed it. Availability is hours per month.
  • Whether a station is usable in practice: entrance placement, distance, staffing and out-of-system transfers are not in this data.
  • Anything about accessibility beyond elevators. Ramps, gap fillers, tactile edges, audio announcements and platform level boarding are not covered.
  • Whether a scheduled replacement is going well or badly. A year of downtime for a capital project and a year of downtime from neglect look identical here.
  • Long Island Rail Road and Metro-North have their own equipment and their own separate feeds. This site is New York City Transit subway only.

Refresh and failure behaviour

Outage durations are computed against Eastern local time, which is how the MTA stamps them; comparing them to a UTC clock inflated every duration by several hours. The fetch script refuses to write a file that is empty or shorter than expected, and it treats a live feed reporting zero outages as a broken feed rather than good news, because across more than 700 units the agency has never reported zero. A failed refresh leaves the previous data in place and exits with an error rather than publishing an empty page.

Code

The fetch and build scripts are in the repository alongside this page. Every figure on the front page can be reproduced by running them against the same public endpoints.
